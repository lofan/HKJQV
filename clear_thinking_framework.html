Thinking, Fast and Slow
Daniel Kahneman · 2011
“Nothing in life is as important as you think it is, while you are thinking about it.”
Core idea
The mind runs on two systems. System 1 is fast, automatic, and emotional — always on. System 2 is slow, deliberate, and effortful — lazy and rarely activated. Most errors occur when System 1 confidently handles problems that require System 2, without notifying you.
Key concepts
Anchoring, availability heuristic, loss aversion (losses hurt ~2× more than equivalent gains feel good), WYSIATI (the mind builds coherent stories from available evidence while ignoring what's missing), regression to the mean, planning fallacy, and the experiencing vs. remembering self.
Practical takeaway
Before any important decision, ask: am I using System 1 when this deserves System 2? Slow down, list counterevidence, and check for loss aversion or anchoring effects.
The Enigma of Reason
Hugo Mercier & Dan Sperber · 2017
“Reason is not a mechanism for discovering truth — it is a mechanism for justifying ourselves to others.”
Core idea
Reason didn't evolve to help individuals think better — it evolved to help groups argue and cooperate. Its primary function is producing and evaluating justifications in social contexts. We're excellent at finding flaws in others' arguments and terrible at spotting flaws in our own.
Key concepts
Myside bias, the argumentative theory of reasoning, lazy intuitions that generate conclusions first with reasons attached after, and why reason works best in genuine dialogue. Groups with diverse views and open debate outperform lone experts.
Practical takeaway
Don't trust solo reasoning on important questions — seek genuine critics. Your best thinking happens when you have to defend a position to someone who genuinely disagrees.
Sources of Power
Gary Klein · 1998
“Experts don't deliberate between options — they recognise situations and act.”
Core idea
The necessary counter to Kahneman. Expert intuition in high-stakes domains is genuinely reliable because experts have built vast pattern libraries from experience. They simulate one option mentally and only consider alternatives if the first feels wrong.
Key concepts
Recognition-Primed Decision (RPD) model, mental simulation as the real unit of expert judgment, and the conditions for trustworthy intuition: a high-validity environment plus years of clear, rapid feedback.
Practical takeaway
Intuition is trustworthy when the environment is regular (surgery, chess) and feedback is fast. Distrust it in irregular, low-feedback environments (politics, markets). Know which domain you're in.
How We Know What Isn't So
Thomas Gilovich · 1991
“The tenacious grip that favoured conclusions have on our credulous minds.”
Core idea
A rigorous catalogue of how people come to believe things that aren't true — not through stupidity but through predictable cognitive machinery. The same processes that make us efficient also make us systematically wrong about patterns and social reality.
Key concepts
The hot hand fallacy (streaks feel meaningful but are largely random), seeing patterns in noise, biased evaluation of evidence (we scrutinise disconfirmatory evidence more harshly), and how social pressure amplifies individual biases into shared misconceptions.
Practical takeaway
When you notice a pattern: what is the base rate? Could this be random? Am I looking for evidence that confirms what I already believe? The most vivid pattern is often the least reliable.

Thinking in Systems
Donella Meadows · 2008
“You can't navigate well in an interconnected, feedback-dominated world unless you take your eyes off short-term events.”
Core idea
Most persistent problems — poverty, pollution, organisational dysfunction — aren't caused by isolated events but by the structure of systems: stocks, flows, and feedback loops. Intervening at the wrong place (a symptom rather than a structure) is why well-intentioned fixes so often make things worse.
Key concepts
Stocks (accumulations) and flows (rates of change), reinforcing feedback loops (exponential growth or collapse), balancing feedback loops (goal-seeking behaviour), delays as the primary cause of oscillation and overshoot, and leverage points — places where a small shift produces large change.
Practical takeaway
Before acting on a problem, draw the system: what are the stocks? Where are the feedback loops? Where are the delays? The highest-leverage interventions are usually counterintuitive — not where the problem appears most visible.
The Model Thinker
Scott Page · 2018
“The more complex the world, the more we need multiple models.”
Core idea
No single model captures reality. Every model simplifies — and what it simplifies away is also what it gets wrong. The solution isn't to find the one true model; it's to hold many simultaneously and triangulate across them. Experts relying on one model are consistently less accurate than those combining many.
Key concepts
Models as logical machines for clarity, not photographic representations of truth. Covers 30+ models including normal distributions, power laws, Markov chains, contagion models, network effects, and game theory. The wisdom of crowds is a many-model phenomenon: diverse independent estimates average out errors.
Practical takeaway
When analysing any problem, explicitly name the model you're using — then name two alternative models and run the same problem through them. Where they disagree is where the real uncertainty lives.

The Demon-Haunted World
Carl Sagan · 1995
“Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence.”
Core idea
Science is not a body of knowledge — it is a method. A way of not deceiving yourself. Scepticism and wonder are not opposites: rigorous doubt is what makes genuine discovery possible. In a world saturated with pseudoscience and motivated reasoning, the scientific method is the most powerful tool humanity has for getting things right.
Key concepts
The baloney detection kit: independent confirmation, debate, quantification, falsifiability, Occam's razor, control experiments, and the distinction between absence of evidence and evidence of absence. Also covers the sociology of pseudoscience — why it spreads and why intelligent people believe it.
Practical takeaway
For any strong claim: Is it falsifiable? Has it been independently replicated? Does it require special pleading to protect it from counterevidence? What would it take to change your mind? If you can't answer that last question, you're rationalising, not reasoning.

Superforecasting
Philip Tetlock · 2015
“Superforecasters treat their opinions as hypotheses to be tested, not treasures to be protected.”
Core idea
Most expert predictions are no better than chance. But a small group of ordinary people — superforecasters — dramatically outperform experts and prediction markets by following specific cognitive habits. Forecasting skill is learnable, and accurate prediction requires treating beliefs as probabilities to be calibrated, not conclusions to be defended.
Key concepts
Calibration (70% confidence calls should be right ~70% of the time), the outside view (start with base rates), decomposition (break hard problems into estimable parts), Bayesian updating (adjust incrementally as evidence arrives), and the fox vs. hedgehog distinction — generalists who aggregate many models beat specialists wedded to one.
Practical takeaway
Before forming a view, find the base rate. State beliefs as explicit probabilities. Track your predictions. Update when evidence arrives — but not too much. Over-reaction to noise is as costly as under-reaction.
How to Lie with Statistics
Darrell Huff · 1954
“The secret language of statistics is employed to sensationalize, inflate, confuse, and oversimplify.”
Core idea
A short, witty manual on every trick used to mislead with numbers — written from the deceiver's perspective so the reader learns to recognise manipulation instantly. Still the most efficient introduction to statistical literacy despite being 70 years old.
Key concepts
The biased sample (who was asked?), the deceptive average (mean vs. median vs. mode tell different stories), the truncated y-axis (inflates apparent change), the semi-attached figure (sounds relevant but measures something else), and post hoc reasoning (correlation presented as causation).
Practical takeaway
For any statistic: Who measured it? Who chose the sample? What does the y-axis start at? Is this correlation or causation? What is the denominator? Asking five questions eliminates 90% of misleading claims.

The Art of Thinking Clearly
Rolf Dobelli · 2011
“The more options you have, the less satisfied you'll be with whichever you choose.”
Core idea
99 cognitive errors in 99 short chapters — each under three pages. Not a theoretical work but a practical field guide. Where Kahneman explains why we err, Dobelli gives you a checklist you can run in real time. Best read after absorbing the theoretical grounding from the other books, as a reference to return to.
Key concepts
Survivorship bias, the swimmer's body illusion (confusing selection for causation), social proof, the sunk cost fallacy, the paradox of choice, the halo effect, the contrast effect, and the narrative fallacy — plus 92 more.
Practical takeaway
Build a personal checklist of your top 10 recurring biases from this book. Before major decisions, run through it. The goal isn't to eliminate bias — it's to catch the specific ones that have burned you before.
The Scout Mindset
Julia Galef · 2021
“The goal of scout mindset isn't to be right — it's to see clearly.”
Core idea
Knowing about biases is not enough. Most people who've read Kahneman still reason badly because they don't actually want to see clearly — they want to feel good, protect their identity, and signal loyalty to their tribe. Clear thinking is primarily a motivational problem, not an intellectual one. The “scout” wants accurate maps; the “soldier” defends territory.
Key concepts
Soldier mindset (using intelligence to defend a pre-chosen conclusion) vs. scout mindset (using intelligence to find out what's true). Identity-protective cognition — beliefs tied to who you are become impervious to evidence. Practical exercises for noticing the moment you switch from scouting to soldiering.
Practical takeaway
Ask yourself: am I asking “is this true?” or “can I believe this?” Notice when being wrong would feel like a personal attack. Decouple your identity from your beliefs — changing them should feel like updating a map, not losing a battle.
